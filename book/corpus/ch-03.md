The protagonist's kitchen, Monday morning, then the office. A handwritten envelope on the doormat. Foreign stamp. They know the handwriting before they know they know it. They open it, read the first line — "You don't have to answer this." — and fold it back along its creases. They put it under the fruit bowl. They do not read the second line.

At work they run a small private calculation: the probability that a letter from fifteen years ago contains anything that requires action. They arrive at a comfortably low number.

Sarah's car has failed its MOT. She mentions it lightly, at the kettle. The protagonist hears themselves offer a lift — "a week or two, I go past yours anyway" — and is appalled. Sarah says yes before they can retract it.

That night they move the fruit bowl two inches to the left so the envelope is fully covered. They go to bed. They do not sleep for a long time, which they attribute to the coffee.
